# Individual reflection: Lazizbek

The thing I'll remember from this project is that writing my predictions down first actually mattered. In PA1 I explained my results after seeing them, and the feedback said I never tested those explanations. This time `predictions.md` was committed before any code existed, so when Stoer-Wagner took 342 ms on a cycle and 560 ms on a dense graph at n = 1024, I couldn't quietly decide that was what I'd expected. I had written that it would be "almost identical across families" because the array version does the same n³ work. It does: the vertex-scan count was 11,250,400 at every m. So the difference had to come from somewhere other than the amount of work.

What I learned is that "the same work" and "the same time" aren't the same claim. Relabelling the same cycle randomly made it 1.6x slower with nothing else changed. A big-O count tells you how many steps there are, not how predictable they are to the CPU. I can't prove the branch-prediction part with my setup, and the report says so, but I wouldn't have thought to look at all if I hadn't been forced to compare against a written prediction.

The second thing was how much the theory hid on the Karger side. The bound 2/(n(n-1)) made me picture Karger as something you run millions of times. On clusters at n = 1024 a single trial worked 91% of the time. The reason turned out to be simple once I counted edges scanned: a trial stops after looking at about 3% of the edges, so it almost never reaches the 3 cross edges. The model built on that idea got the direction right but only fell inside the confidence interval in 7 of 22 cases. I'd rather report that honestly than claim it fully explains the data. The same counter showed that most of a Karger trial's time goes into shuffling edges it never uses. That's a concrete improvement I'd make next.

On testing, the attack prompts changed how I think about "done". My Karger passed 300 random graphs against brute force and still segfaulted on n = 0 and returned a min cut of 0 for a single vertex. Neither is a case I'd have thought to test, and writing the failing test before the fix made the history show the bug was real. The generator bug was the same lesson in a different place: the clusters generator promised a min cut of 3, and at small n brute force found 1. If I hadn't tested the generator, a whole family of results would have been measuring the wrong cut.

On AI, I should be honest that Claude wrote most of the code and drafted most of the writing, and my job was to direct it, run everything on my machine, check the output and commit step by step. That worked well for speed, but it meant I had to make a deliberate effort to understand each piece rather than just accept files that already passed their tests. The hand traces in `notes.md` and stepping through Stoer-Wagner phase by phase were where that understanding actually happened. The times the AI was wrong were all things that looked right until something was measured. That's the same lesson as the predictions: the claim isn't true until something checks it.

If I did this again, I'd look at the operation counts from the start instead of adding them once times looked strange. The counters ended up explaining more than the timings did.
